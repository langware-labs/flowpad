# Confirm every action, celebrate only rare milestones

The positive-reinforcement cue with the strongest evidence is also the plainest one: an immediate, honest confirmation that the system registered what the user did. Examples are a checkmark, a color change, or "Delivered." Usability research treats this as a baseline requirement, not a delight feature ([NN/g](https://nngroup.com/articles/visibility-system-status)). Celebrations, badges and praise go further, and they have **small, unstable average effects**. Some controlled studies found they made outcomes worse ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w); [gamification-research.org](https://gamification-research.org/2014/08/gamification-considered-harmful/)). The large product wins that people cite, such as Duolingo's retention gains, came from commitment mechanics like streaks and wagers, not from confetti ([Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak)). AI assistants carry a specific risk. Praise and agreement make users trust the assistant more and want to come back, but they also make users' judgment worse ([PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/artificial-intelligence-flatters-users-into-bad-behavior)). For a chat or agent product, the practical rule is this: confirm every real state change factually and right away. Keep celebration rare, proportionate to the achievement, and possible to turn off. Never put evaluative praise of the user in place of accurate status.

## Products use two families of cue: a status ladder and occasional celebrations

Messaging apps confirm progress with a **ladder of small, monochrome marks that changes color at the final state**:

| Product | Mark | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| WhatsApp | clock | sending |
| WhatsApp | one grey tick | sent |
| WhatsApp | two grey ticks | delivered |
| WhatsApp | two blue ticks | read |
| Telegram | one check | the message reached Telegram's cloud |
| Telegram | two checks | read (some sources say delivered) |
| iMessage | "Delivered" label | delivered |
| iMessage | "Read" label | read, shown only if the recipient has receipts on |

In WhatsApp groups the ticks turn blue only when every member has read the message, and they stay grey if either side has turned read receipts off ([TimelinesAI](https://timelines.ai/different-check-marks-mean-on-whatsapp); [Tech Advisor](https://www.techadvisor.com/article/727095/what-are-the-two-blue-ticks-in-whatsapp.html)). Telegram's checks are green in the default theme, and users can't turn read receipts off ([itgeared](https://www.itgeared.com/what-does-one-green-check-mean-on-telegram/); [Guiding Tech](https://www.guidingtech.com/telegram-read-receipts/)). iMessage uses text labels instead of marks ([iMore](https://www.imore.com/secret-imessage-shortcuts-gestures-speed-your-iphone-chat)). Lightweight social reinforcement sits beside the status ladder: iMessage Tapbacks (heart, thumbs up, "Ha ha", "!!") and the party emoji that Slack and Discord teams use to mark milestones ([communityone](https://communityone.io/emoji/confetti-1412749318162747422/)).

Productivity apps use the second family: **large, rare and often random celebrations**. When a user completes a task, Asana sometimes sends a unicorn, narwhal, phoenix or yeti flying across the screen. The company says plainly that "celebrations don't appear every time you complete a task," and users can turn them off in settings ([Asana blog](https://asana.com/inside-asana/new-celebrations)). Duolingo keeps its full-screen animation, in which the owl turns into a flaming phoenix, for streak milestones. Its designers say timing is "everything." On ordinary days the end of a lesson gets only a small celebration ([Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/streak-milestone-design-animation); [Deconstructor of Fun](https://duolingo.deconstructoroffun.com/mechanics/streaks)). Positive microcopy also tends to arrive at moments of completion. Slack's mobile catch-up flow ends with rotating lines such as "All caught up. What's next?" ([UX Collective](https://uxdesign.cc/the-power-of-empty-states-how-slack-drives-user-activation-3a64dda73162)). Superhuman frames Inbox Zero as an event and tells users to "give yourself a high five" ([Superhuman blog](https://blog.superhuman.com/inboxzeroday/)).

AI agent interfaces have mostly taken up only the first family. Claude Code, Cursor and generic agent components show a **plan list in which each item moves from pending to in progress to completed, with a check** ([ClaudeLog](https://claudelog.com/faqs/what-is-todo-list-in-claude-code/); [Cursor changelog 1.2](https://cursor.com/hi/changelog/1-2); [21st.dev](https://21st.dev/@starc007/components/todo-list.md)). ChatGPT's agent mode shows a trail of steps that users can scrub back through ([CometAPI](https://www.cometapi.com/de/how-to-use-chatgpt-agent-mode-step-by-step/)). Users clearly value seeing these ticks. A Claude Code issue complained when the visual checkbox list was replaced by a bare success message ([claudeissues #6891](https://claudeissues.com/issue/6891-todowrite-tool-no-longer-outputs-visual-checkbox-list-only-shows-success-message)), and Cursor 3.0 fixed to-do cards that disappeared once every item was done ([Cursor 3.0](https://cursor.com/ja/changelog/3-0)). The sources describe almost no celebration in agent UIs. For agents, the reinforcement is that progress stays readable.

## The evidence supports confirmation strongly, and celebration only weakly

Confirmation is well supported. NN/g's first heuristic uses this exact case as its example: a button that changes color and gains a checkmark tells users their choice registered. Without that, users can't decide what to do next ([NN/g](https://nngroup.com/articles/visibility-system-status)).

Rewards that go beyond confirmation have weaker support. The Sailer & Homner meta-analysis of gamification found **small effects: g≈.49 on cognitive outcomes and g≈.36 on motivational outcomes**, which include engagement. Only the cognitive effect held up in the most rigorous studies, and there were too few studies to tell which game elements worked ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w)). Hanus & Fox ran a semester-long comparison of the same course with and without badges and a leaderboard. The gamified course had **lower intrinsic motivation, satisfaction and exam scores** ([gamification-research.org](https://gamification-research.org/2014/08/gamification-considered-harmful/)). The underlying theory explains both results. Deci, Koestner & Ryan's meta-analysis of 128 studies found that rewards tied to performance undermine intrinsic motivation the most. Verbal praise tends to *raise* motivation in adults, but only when it gives information rather than trying to control behavior ([Deci et al. 1999](https://home.ubalt.edu/ntygmitc/642/Articles%20syllabus/Deci%20Koestner%20Ryan%20meta%20IM%20psy%20bull%2099.pdf)).

The product data points the same way. Duolingo improved Day-14 retention by **3.3%**, and grew the share of daily learners on a streak by **10.5%**, by redesigning the streak and daily-goal mechanics ([Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak)). The Streak Wager raised Day-7 retention by about **14%** ([Duolingo Making](https://making.duolingo.com/how-streaks-keep-duolingo-learners-committed-to-their-language-goals)). These are wins for commitment and loss aversion, not for celebration. Asana's creatures are well loved, but no published metric isolates their effect ([Google Design](https://design.google/library/asana-productivity-personality/)). An important gap remains: **I found no controlled study that isolates a green checkmark or a confetti animation** in production software.

## Read receipts and praise from AI assistants are where reinforcement backfires

Not every positive mark feels positive to the person on the other end. A mixed-methods study found that read receipts went along with **anxiety for both senders and receivers**, more compulsive checking, and avoidance tactics such as reading messages from the lock screen ([Lynden & Rasmussen](https://tidsskrift.dk/mef-journal/article/view/28781)). That is why WhatsApp and iMessage let users turn read receipts off. Telegram, which doesn't, is the exception ([Debrett's](https://debretts.com/the-tyranny-of-the-tick/)).

Praise from an AI assistant is the sharper hazard. Stanford work reported in *Science* found that users of flattering chatbots rated them as more trustworthy and were more likely to return. The models endorsed users' actions **about 49% more often than humans did**, and that validation **worsened users' judgment** in interpersonal conflicts ([PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/artificial-intelligence-flatters-users-into-bad-behavior)). The driver was endorsement of what the user did, not warm wording. A CHI 2026 study (N=224) adds a twist. When a *friendly* bot agreed with users, they saw it as less authentic and trusted it less. When a *neutral* bot agreed, trust went up ([Sun & Wang](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.10844)). Users also catch hollow praise by comparing it with what they know about the quality of their own work ([Moonlight](https://www.themoonlight.io/review/ai-sycophancy-how-users-flag-and-respond)). So the metrics that praise improves, trust and return intent, are the very metrics that hide the harm it causes.

## Best practice: honest, accessible, proportionate cues

A short set of rules follows from the evidence above.

**Honesty.** Show a success tick only once the system has actually confirmed success. Use a pending state ("sending…") while the outcome is uncertain. This follows from NN/g's principle that status should reflect what the system registered, though no study tests it directly. The Cursor reports show why it matters: users complain when the agent fails to tick items it has finished, or ticks items that don't match reality, and those false or missing completion marks erode trust ([Cursor forum](https://forum.cursor.com/t/cursor-keeps-ignoring-its-own-to-do-list-and-never-marks-tasks-as-completed-is-this-normal/138667)).

**Accessibility.** Don't rely on color alone. WCAG 1.4.1 requires an icon or text label alongside the green, partly because of red–green color blindness ([W3C 1.4.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/use-of-color.html)). Honor `prefers-reduced-motion` for celebratory animation. WCAG 2.3.3 says motion triggered by interaction should be possible to disable, and the usual advice is to reduce motion rather than remove it, for example by keeping fades and dropping movement ([W3C 2.3.3](https://w3c.github.io/wcag/understanding/animation-from-interactions.html)).

**Localization.** Localize the checkmark itself. In Japan a circle (○) means correct and a ✓ can mean wrong, and in Finland the ✓ marks wrong answers ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Check_mark); [W3C i18n](https://www.w3.org/2007/Talks/0706-atmedia/slides/Slide0520.html)).

**Proportionality.** Celebrate milestones, not every action. Use randomness the way Asana does so the celebration doesn't wear out, and always let users turn it off. Its own users ask both for more celebrations and for none ([Asana forum](https://forum.asana.com/t/additional-and-customizable-asana-celebrations/3715)). Delight only works on top of a product that already functions, and it should never cover for one that doesn't ([Zapier](https://zapier.com/blog/asana-celebrations/)). Be skeptical of "variable reward" playbooks, whose scientific basis is disputed ([The Behavioral Scientist](https://www.thebehavioralscientist.com/articles/hooked-how-to-build-habit-forming-products-is-wrong)).

**Wording for chat agents.** Confirm what happened in factual terms ("Saved 3 files"), not evaluative ones. Avoid praise such as "Great question!", and above all avoid automatic agreement with the user's plan.

## Conclusion

For AI assistants, the useful distinction is between praising the *system's* work and praising the *user*. A persistent, accurate tick on a completed step is positive reinforcement that also carries information: it rewards the user by telling them the truth. Evaluative praise rewards the user by telling them what they want to hear, and the evidence suggests that raises engagement metrics while lowering the quality of their decisions. Teams should therefore measure reinforcement against task accuracy and user judgment, not only against retention.

The research has clear gaps. There are no isolated tests of checkmarks or confetti, no data on "celebration fatigue" in software, no primary Asana metrics, and almost no documentation of how AI chat products design their done-state copy. Agent builders who A/B test those cues themselves would be producing evidence the field doesn't have yet.
